## Executive Summary

On August 5, 2026, the Rust project published a policy on its Inside Rust blog governing how large language models may be used in contributions to the `rust-lang/rust` compiler repository: LLMs may "answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, review," but contributors may not use them to author code, comments, or PR descriptions that get posted publicly — and any LLM involvement that is posted must be disclosed. It is the newest entry in a two-year sequence of open-source governance decisions that runs from Gentoo's blanket ban in April 2024 through Fedora's disclosure-based framework, the Linux kernel's `Assisted-by:` tagging convention, and Ghostty's "zero-tolerance… anti-idiot" policy. None of these projects are anti-AI as a matter of ideology. Nearly all of them are responding to the same underlying pressure: contribution volume has decoupled from reviewer capacity, and the traditional proxy for a contributor's effort and competence — a plausible-looking patch — no longer means what it used to. At the same time, some organizations, most visibly Anthropic, report that a supermajority of their own shipped code is now AI-authored, sustained entirely by human review gates rather than contributor-side restriction. The real fault line in 2026 is not "AI or no AI" — it's who absorbs the review burden and who is accountable when something breaks.

## The Rust Policy: Narrower and More Careful Than the Headlines Suggest

The policy, written by Jynn Nelson and published on [Inside Rust](https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2026/08/05/rust-langrust-is-adopting-an-llm-policy/) — the team-facing blog, distinct from Rust's main release blog — applies only to `rust-lang/rust`, the compiler monorepo, and only to the teams that ratified it: compiler, libs, types, rustdoc, and bootstrap. It explicitly does not cover crates.io, docs.rs, the RFCs repo, or teams that didn't sign on, such as lang. A separate attempt at a project-wide policy, tracked as [rust-lang/rfcs#3959](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs), failed to reach consensus — this is a scoped compromise, not an official Rust Project stance on AI.

The core rule, quoted directly from the post: "It's fine to use LLMs to answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, review… But not to create." Private use — asking an LLM questions, drafting throwaway experiments you never publish — requires no disclosure at all. What crosses the line is publishing LLM output: non-trivial doc comments, safety comments, or PR/issue text generated by a model, and especially treating an LLM's review as a substitute for a human's. There is one narrow exception: LLM-authored code changes are allowed experimentally when pre-arranged with a reviewer, non-critical to soundness, held to the same or higher quality bar as human code, and both parties can demonstrably explain it. Notably, the policy has no mechanical disclosure format — no PR-template checkbox, no required commit trailer — disclosure is simply a written obligation, enforced socially rather than by tooling.

The stated rationale is threefold: polished output no longer signals effort or understanding ("signal degradation"); the compiler team already carries a large open-PR backlog (the post cites roughly 1,281 open PRs) and LLMs increase submission volume without increasing reviewer capacity; and round-tripping review feedback through a model wastes everyone's time and breeds mutual suspicion. Pointedly, the policy bars PR comments from litigating the copyright status of LLM output, its environmental impact, or moral judgments about LLM users — treating those as off-topic for a code-review venue rather than as grounds for the policy itself.

Enforcement leans on trust rather than surveillance: reviewers may close non-compliant PRs "no questions asked," but moderators are explicitly told "it's not your job to play detective" — writing style alone isn't evidence of AI use, a provision that drew notable praise in the Hacker News discussion of the announcement ([news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49179039](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49179039), ~108 points). Deliberately lying about LLM use is treated as a Code of Conduct violation. A "circuit breaker" caps things structurally: if more than half of merged PRs in any rolling six-week window are LLM-created, new LLM-authored PRs are disallowed until the ratio drops back, with a mandatory ten-day cooldown. An earlier Hacker News thread that tracked the policy while it was still under review ([item?id=48142650](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48142650), ~100 points) was more split — some commenters called the eventual rule performative and unenforceable, and worried the "pre-arranged with a reviewer" carve-out would favor already-trusted contributors. Rust's own language-design community is reportedly not unanimous either: secondary reporting attributes to Niko Matsakis the view that the policy forecloses conversation with future LLM-native contributors before the project has evidence to justify restricting them — a claim worth treating cautiously, since it traces to aggregated coverage rather than a primary Zulip or GitHub quote this research could independently verify.

## The Spectrum: From Outright Bans to Conditional Welcome

Rust's approach sits in the middle of a spectrum that has been forming since 2024, and every entry below is checked against the project's own policy text or governance record.

**Outright bans.** Gentoo's Council voted 6–0 on April 14, 2024 to forbid "any content that has been created with the assistance of Natural Language Processing artificial intelligence tools" in official contributions ([wiki.gentoo.org/wiki/Project:Council/AI_policy](https://wiki.gentoo.org/wiki/Project:Council/AI_policy)), citing copyright risk, "plausible-looking nonsense," and ethical concerns about the tooling itself. NetBSD followed a month later, on May 15, 2024, amending its commit guidelines so that LLM-generated code "is presumed to be tainted code, and must not be committed without prior written approval by core" — a ban with a narrow override, not an absolute one ([netbsd.org/developers/commit-guidelines.html](https://www.netbsd.org/developers/commit-guidelines.html)). Servo adopted a similarly hard line on June 27, 2024 — "Contributions must not include content generated by large language models" — and reaffirmed it in April 2025 after a proposal to pilot Copilot was rejected by the Technical Steering Committee following heavy community pushback (356 downvotes to 18 upvotes on the discussion thread). QEMU banned AI-derived contributions on June 16, 2025, reasoning that contributors cannot credibly certify the Developer's Certificate of Origin for output they didn't actually write ([qemu.org/docs/master/devel/code-provenance.html](https://www.qemu.org/docs/master/devel/code-provenance.html)) — though a relaxation patch permitting small, mechanical AI-assisted changes was proposed by Paolo Bonzini in May 2026 and had not visibly merged as of this writing.

**Disclosure-required, conditional allowance.** This is now the most common posture among large, actively governed projects, and it's where Rust, Fedora, the Linux kernel, and Kubernetes all land. Fedora's Council approved a policy on October 22, 2025 stating "the contributor is always the author and is fully accountable" and requiring disclosure "when the significant part of the contribution is taken from a tool without changes" — while adding pointedly that "AI should not make the final determination on whether a contribution is accepted or not." The Linux kernel formalized its stance in two documents committed in January 2026: `Documentation/process/coding-assistants.rst`, which states flatly that "AI agents MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags. Only humans can legally certify the Developer Certificate of Origin," and requires an `Assisted-by: AGENT_NAME:MODEL_VERSION` disclosure tag; and `Documentation/process/generated-content.rst`, which asks contributors to include the prompts that produced heavily-generated content. Notably, this project-wide stance is not a ban — Linus Torvalds has publicly called AI "clearly a useful tool" — but it coexists with a much harder rule in one high-noise subsystem: on August 3, 2026, Greg Kroah-Hartman announced on the kernel mailing list that he will "automatically reject any patches generated with a LLM for the drivers/staging/ subsystem," adding "it is VERY obvious when people submit LLM-generated patches" ([lore.kernel.org/lkml](https://lore.kernel.org/lkml/2026080354-skater-urgent-31b2@gregkh/)). Kubernetes adopted a comparable disclosure rule on June 26, 2026: "Contributors cannot rely on AI to respond to review comments. If you cannot personally explain changes that AI helped generate, your PR will be closed" ([kubernetes.io/blog](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2026/06/26/open-source-maintainership-in-the-age-of-ai/)) — notably scoped to the Kubernetes project itself; no CNCF-wide policy was found.

**Escalating restriction on autonomous agents specifically.** Ghostty and Home Assistant both distinguish AI-assisted humans (fine) from autonomous agents operating with no meaningful human in the loop (not fine), and both have hardened their rules over time. Ghostty's Mitchell Hashimoto moved from a disclosure requirement in August 2025 to a much stricter rule in January 2026: "AI generated contributions will only be allowed for accepted issues and maintainers. Drive-by pull requests with AI generated content will be immediately closed… users who contribute bad AI generated content will be immediately banned. This is a zero-tolerance policy" — paired with the now-widely-quoted line, "This is not an anti-AI stance. This is an anti-idiot stance." Home Assistant's policy, published July 20–21, 2026, is more categorical about agents specifically: "We do not allow autonomous agents to be used for contributing to our projects. We will close any pull requests or issues that we believe were created autonomously." Mozilla's Firefox policy, by contrast, requires no disclosure at all and instead makes accountability the whole test: "AI can assist, but responsibility always stays with the human behind the change… You're expected to understand and be able to explain every change you make."

**Conditional welcome.** The clearest verified example of a project actively inviting AI-assisted contributions is Hugging Face's `transformers`, whose contributing guide has a section titled "Agentic contributions": "AI-assisted contributions are welcome. They must be coordinated, scoped, and verified to keep review load manageable." But the gates are real — no PR may be "pure agent" output with no human line-by-line review, tools must be disclosed along with test results, and first-time contributors are explicitly barred from opening agent-authored PRs at all: "PRs that appear to be agent-written will probably be closed without review." Across every project surveyed here, no credible major project was found operating a genuinely frictionless, ungated "agent PRs welcome" policy. The real divide in 2026 is not ban-versus-welcome; it's ban-versus-gated-permission, and even the permissive end of that spectrum is gated.

**Live and moving.** Two data points are worth flagging as still in motion: Debian has six competing General Resolution proposals on LLM usage under a formal vote, with discussion running through August 8, 2026 — a decision that may land within days of this article; and QEMU's relaxation proposal remains unmerged. Anyone citing these positions later should recheck them.

## Why Maintainers Are Pulling Back: Copyright, DCO, and the Slop Tax

Three distinct concerns recur across nearly every policy, though they're weighted differently project to project.

**Copyright and license provenance.** The Software Freedom Conservancy's guidance deliberately avoids declaring AI output's copyright status settled, while insisting that copyleft obligations survive LLM mediation regardless: "if that codebase is under a copyleft license, your changes must be licensed under the project's license." The Free Software Foundation commissioned white papers specifically on whether models trained on GPL code produce derivative works, flagging the question as legally open. In practice, this uncertainty shows up less as a stated *reason* for a policy and more as background pressure that makes maintainers reluctant to accept contributions whose authorship chain they can't fully vouch for.

**DCO and CLA meaning.** This is where the reasoning gets sharpest. QEMU's ban is explicitly framed around the Developer's Certificate of Origin: a contributor cannot honestly certify DCO provenance for text an LLM produced. The Linux kernel's answer is structural rather than prohibitive — it keeps the DCO meaningful precisely by reserving it for humans: "AI agents MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags," so the human submitter absorbs full legal and technical responsibility by signing off on AI-touched code as their own reviewed work, not by claiming authorship of the AI's output.

**Review-burden asymmetry — the "AI slop" tax.** This is the most concrete, best-documented driver, and curl is the richest case study available. Maintainer Daniel Stenberg has published a near-continuous record on daniel.haxx.se: by mid-2025, roughly 20% of curl's bug-bounty submissions were AI-generated slop, and the confirmed-vulnerability rate had fallen to about 5%, against a baseline of 87 genuine vulnerabilities and over $100,000 paid out since 2019. "Each report thus engages 3-4 persons," he wrote, "sometimes up to an hour or three. Each." The bounty program was terminated at the end of January 2026 specifically "to remove the incentive for people to submit crap and non-well researched reports." Tellingly, Stenberg didn't become anti-AI — by April 2026 he reported the opposite trend, that AI-assisted security research had become genuinely high quality once the monetary incentive for junk was removed, crediting one AI-assisted researcher with roughly 50 merged bugfixes. The Python collective Jazzband cited a parallel "1 in 10 AI-generated PRs meets project standards" figure when it announced it was sunsetting its shared-maintenance model in March 2026, explicitly referencing curl's numbers.

GitHub's own maintainer-facing data corroborates the volume story independent of quality: monthly merged PRs across the platform grew from roughly 25 million in January 2023 to over 90 million by June 2026 — a 3.6x increase the company partly attributes to AI-assisted contribution — prompting GitHub to ship PR-limiting and collaborator-only settings in the first half of 2026. Multiple projects besides curl closed their doors to public contribution in response during the same window: Ladybird's Andreas Kling wrote in June 2026 that "a substantial patch used to imply substantial effort, and that effort was a reasonable proxy for good faith. That assumption no longer holds"; the `openai/codex` project went invitation-only in January 2026 because "external submissions had become a bottleneck rather than a force multiplier." On code quality specifically, GitClear's longitudinal analysis of 211 million changed lines found that copy-pasted code overtook refactored code for the first time in 2024, while a 2025 academic study of self-disclosed GenAI usage on GitHub (arXiv 2507.10422) found no significant quality difference between disclosed-AI and human-only contributions — a genuine tension in the evidence that this survey did not find fully resolved.

## The Counter-Current: Agent-Authored Code at Production Scale

None of this has stopped organizations from running agent-authored code at real scale internally — the difference is that the review gate moves inside a trusted organizational boundary instead of sitting at the open contribution edge. Anthropic's own account, published July 21, 2026, states that Claude now authors roughly 80% of code merged into Anthropic's codebase, a figure corroborated independently by VentureBeat and Winbuzzer coverage. The architecture that makes this tenable is explicitly a review-gate stack, not an honor system: multiple narrow-scoped automated reviewer agents, a mandatory `/security-review` pass, SAST tooling posting directly on PRs, risk-weighted routing of some findings to mandatory human review, and — critically — "every approval is logged with the signals and reasoning behind it," with human final approval required throughout. Google's Cloud CTO office described a parallel structure in April 2026: AI-generated test coverage requirements, "Conditional LGTM" approvals gated on passing tests, and a "Zero-Trust Model" requiring permission checks before an agent can execute a tool — alongside the observation that "the bug-to-code ratio remains constant… only the timeline changes," a useful reframing that the real cost of agent-authored code is review load, not necessarily defect rate.

On the mechanics of disclosure, conventions are converging but not yet standardized. Claude Code appends a `Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>` trailer to commits by default (configurable), reusing the pre-existing git co-author convention rather than inventing a new one; GitHub Copilot's coding agent goes further, authoring commits directly with the initiating human listed as co-author and the commit cryptographically signed "Verified." This has not been friction-free — a GitHub issue against Claude Code (#66602) argued the default trailer sits awkwardly against U.S. Copyright Office guidance that AI tools should not be listed as authors or co-authors — and Microsoft reportedly walked back a similar default in VS Code's Copilot after community pushback. The most concrete emerging disclosure standard is the Linux kernel's `Assisted-by:` tag; the Apache Software Foundation has proposed a comparable `Generated-by:` commit token and backed it with a $10M Responsible AI Initiative launched in early 2026. Beyond that, proposals exist — notably Andrew Nesbitt's May 2026 "RFC: Artificial Contributors to Open Source" — but these are independent proposals written in RFC-style language, not ratified standards; no evidence surfaced of actual IETF or W3C engagement on AI contribution disclosure as of this writing.

## What This Means for Teams Running AI Agents

For a team whose agents author pull requests as a matter of daily workflow, the pattern across two years of open-source governance decisions points to a few concrete operating rules.

**Treat "which upstream" as a policy lookup, not an assumption.** The spectrum above is wide enough that a single blanket internal rule ("agents may contribute upstream") will be wrong somewhere. Outright-ban projects (Gentoo, NetBSD, Servo, QEMU, Ghostty for drive-bys, Home Assistant for autonomous agents) will close agent-authored PRs on sight regardless of quality — contributing there means an agent can draft, but a human must be the visible author and understand every line before it's submitted, with no disclosure trail suggesting otherwise. Disclosure-required projects (Rust, Fedora, the kernel outside staging, Kubernetes) are viable targets for agent-assisted contribution provided disclosure is honest and a human can answer review questions — which in practice means the human-in-the-loop step has to happen *before* submission, not as a rubber stamp after.

**Human review is the actual compliance boundary, not the disclosure trailer.** Nearly every policy surveyed — Fedora's "AI should not make the final determination," Mozilla's "you're expected to understand and be able to explain every change," Kubernetes' "if you cannot personally explain changes… your PR will be closed," `transformers`' ban on "pure agent" PRs — converges on the same test: can a specific accountable human explain and stand behind this change. A team running agents should build that gate as a real step (a human reads the diff, runs it, can answer a reviewer's question without going back to the agent) rather than as a checkbox, because maintainers on the other end are explicitly watching for round-tripped, un-understood output — Rust's policy calls out mechanically pasting review feedback into an LLM and pasting the response back as the specific behavior that erodes trust fastest.

**Disclosure norms are inconsistent enough that "always disclose, honestly and specifically" is the safest default.** Some projects want a trailer (`Assisted-by:`, proposed `Generated-by:`), some want prose in the PR description, and some (Mozilla) want none — but every project that has been burned by silent AI contribution treats *concealment* as the worse offense, and Rust's policy makes lying about LLM use a Code of Conduct violation distinct from ordinary noncompliance. Defaulting to over-disclosure, with enough specificity that a maintainer isn't left guessing which parts were AI-touched, degrades gracefully across every policy regime surveyed here.

**Expect the review-burden argument to keep tightening the rules, not loosening them.** The trajectory across 2024–2026 is not toward normalization — it's toward more subsystems and more projects adding restrictions (the kernel's staging ban is four days old as of this article), even as the aggregate volume of AI-assisted contribution keeps climbing. A team whose agents contribute upstream regularly should treat each target project's policy as something to recheck periodically, not something to memorize once — curl's own posture changed twice in fifteen months as the underlying problem (indiscriminate slop) gave way to a different one (high-quality AI-assisted contribution once the incentive to spam was removed).
