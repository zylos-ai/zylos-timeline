## Executive Summary

Agent lifecycle management — provisioning, monitoring, and eventually retiring an AI agent — is the part of agentic AI adoption that has fallen furthest behind deployment speed. Enterprises are running more agents than they can inventory (an average of roughly 37 per organization by late 2025, with some running into the hundreds), while fewer than one in five have a mature governance model for retiring them cleanly. The dominant failure pattern is the "ghost agent": an automation, bot, or credential left running after the person who built it leaves, because offboarding checklists were designed for humans with a single SSO identity, not for agents whose credentials, memory, and downstream integrations fan out across systems nobody remembers provisioning. 2026 is the year this started to close — Microsoft, Okta, and others shipped the first purpose-built agent registries and retirement workflows — but data archival practice (what happens to an agent's memory, logs, and embeddings after retirement) and knowledge-continuity practice (how a successor agent inherits what mattered) remain far less standardized than identity governance. This matters directly for us: we just decommissioned zylos100 and wrote our own archival standard before any of this literature existed for us to check ourselves against. The rest of this piece surveys where the industry actually stands, so that standard can be judged against real practice rather than intuition.

## Current State: Adoption Has Outrun Governance

The clearest fact about agent lifecycle management in 2026 is a gap, and it is a measured one, not a vibe. IBM's 2026 Tech Leader Study found 77% of organizations say AI adoption is already outpacing their governance capability. Gravitee's *State of AI Agent Security 2026* report puts a sharper edge on it: the average organization ran roughly 37 agents by December 2025, and 38% of organizations had crossed 100+ agents by April 2026 — yet only 14.4% of live agents went to production with full security and IT sign-off, and only 47.1% are actively monitored at all. 88% of organizations reported a confirmed or suspected AI agent security incident in the past year, while 82% of executives reported confidence in their protections anyway. Salesforce's 2026 Connectivity Benchmark projects Fortune 500 companies will run roughly 150,000 AI agents by 2028, up from fewer than 15 in 2025 — a scale jump that turns lifecycle management from a nice-to-have into infrastructure.

The retirement side is where the gap is worst. 1Kosmos has given the pattern a name — the "Ghost Agent Problem" — and estimates the average enterprise environment contains 800+ risky AI agents, 40% carrying medium-to-critical risk factors, with only 15% of organizations having anything close to full agent-ownership visibility. CloudEagle.ai's research found 48% of companies had former employees retaining access to active tools, and identified five agent types that "routinely survive offboarding": Copilot Studio agents, Zapier-style automation workflows, embedded API keys, platform-native agents (e.g., built inside Salesforce), and MCP server connections — all because their credential (an OAuth token, API key, or service principal) was provisioned independently of the employee's SSO login, so disabling SSO does nothing to them. OWASP's Non-Human Identity Top 10 (2025) puts "Improper Offboarding" at #1 for exactly this reason, in an environment where non-human identities already outnumber humans roughly 100:1.

## Key Approaches: Four Places the Industry Is Building Structure

**Identity-first retirement tooling.** The most concrete 2026 progress has come from IAM vendors treating agents as first-class identity principals rather than service accounts. Microsoft's Agent 365, GA as of May 1, 2026, is explicit about the framing: agents "get hired, onboarded, put to work, monitored, and eventually off-boarded when they're no longer needed." Its Agent Registry surfaces an "agents without owners" view — flagging agents whose creator has left — and lets admins reassign ownership or block/unblock/delete an agent outright. Microsoft Entra Agent ID reached general availability in April 2026, extending the existing Zero Trust stack to cover agents specifically. Okta's parallel effort, Cross App Access (XAA), launched at Showcase 2026 as an open protocol for agent identity federation, with 25+ early adopters including Anthropic, Atlassian, and Slack. Salesforce Agentforce takes a lighter approach — simple activate/deactivate toggles tied to committed agent versions. Notably, the popular open-source orchestration frameworks (LangGraph, AutoGen, CrewAI) do not yet publish explicit retirement or decommissioning features of their own — this layer is currently being built by identity platforms sitting above the frameworks, not by the frameworks themselves.

**Tiered data retention for archived agent data.** An emerging convention, echoed across several vendor and compliance writeups, is a two-tier pattern: 30–90 days of "hot" storage for active debugging and analysis, followed by cold archival (object storage tiers like S3/Glacier or Azure Blob cold tier) for compliance and historical retrieval at lower cost. Regulated industries set the floor higher — HIPAA-adjacent healthcare logs at 6–7 years, SOX-adjacent financial records at 5–7 years — while GDPR pushes the other direction, capping most operational logs at 90 days absent specific justification. The practical implication for multi-agent systems is that a single retention policy rarely fits: an agent's operational logs, its long-term memory store, and any regulated data it touched in passing may each need different retention treatment, decided at design time rather than retrofitted at decommission time.

**The memory/vector-store blind spot.** A recurring and under-addressed point in the research is that deleting an agent's front end does not delete its memory. In architectures where a memory layer or vector store is decoupled from the conversational interface — which is now the common pattern — the embeddings, metadata, and searchable indexes can persist indefinitely after the agent itself is switched off, and together they can reconstruct substantially all of the original interactions even if raw transcripts were never stored. Current best practice for migrating or archiving agent memory is not to migrate the vectors themselves but to migrate the underlying text and let the destination system regenerate its own embeddings — vectors are treated as a derived, regenerable artifact, not a portable one. For a decommissioned agent that will not be migrated anywhere, this means archival has to explicitly account for the memory layer as a separate system from the agent's compute/runtime, with its own deletion or archival step — it will not go away just because the agent's process does.

**Knowledge continuity as a structured handoff, not a data dump.** The research literature on transferring context between agents (or between an outgoing and incoming agent) converges on a specific failure mode: the "context dump fallacy," where practitioners assume more raw context transferred is better, when in practice large unstructured dumps add noise and strip out the *reasoning* behind past decisions, leaving a successor agent unable to tell why something was done a certain way even though it has the record that it was done. The proposed fixes share a shape: structure the transfer artifact around decisions and their rationale, not raw logs. Academic frameworks like MemOS treat memory as a governed resource with provenance and versioning attached to each unit (its "MemCube"). Lighter-weight practitioner patterns like "capsules" propose tiered handoff documents — a quick capsule that just informs, a standard capsule that instructs, a deep capsule that aims to let the successor replicate prior behavior. The common thread across both academic and practitioner approaches is that a good handoff preserves *why*, and a bad one preserves only *what*.

## Challenges

**Governance frameworks exist at the wrong altitude.** NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (Govern/Map/Measure/Manage) and ISO/IEC 5338 (AI system lifecycle, explicitly spanning "design and development to deployment, operation, and eventual decommissioning") both name decommissioning as in-scope, but neither specifies concrete archival mechanics — retention windows, memory-layer handling, credential revocation sequencing. They set expectations that an agent should have a defined owner, scope, and lifecycle; they don't yet tell an implementer what to actually do with the vector store on the day an agent is shut off. That operational detail is currently being worked out ad hoc, by individual vendors and individual teams, rather than standardized.

**Multi-agent systems compound the leakage surface.** In a multi-agent deployment, conversation and log data doesn't stay confined to one agent's boundary — an authentication agent may log credentials during troubleshooting, a database agent may log query results containing PII, a file-handling agent may log document contents including trade secrets. Archival policy written for a single agent's logs understates the real exposure once several agents' logs are considered together, because the sensitive-data footprint is the union across agents, not any one agent's individual log stream.

**Data sovereignty is an architecture decision, not a location fact.** Current framing treats "data sovereignty" for agents as something enforced at the context layer (regional context partitioning, RBAC-scoped context filtering, audit trails on context access) rather than something satisfied merely by choosing a data-residency region. Multi-agent pipelines routinely span GPU clouds, training systems, and inference endpoints in ways that break location-based compliance reasoning — an agent's memory can be technically hosted in-region while still being reachable, copyable, or replicable in ways that defeat the intent of a residency requirement. This is directly relevant to archival: where an archive physically sits matters less than who and what can still query it.

**Institutional knowledge loss is the default, not the exception.** Gartner's widely cited estimate that 70–80% of enterprise knowledge is tacit — never written down or independently retrievable — applies with extra force to agents, because an agent's "tacit knowledge" includes not just facts but accumulated judgment about edge cases, exceptions, and why a given approach was chosen over an equally plausible alternative. Without a deliberate mechanism to capture that (decision traces, rationale-bearing handoff documents), a successor agent starts from the static rules and documentation that happened to get written down, not from the full reasoning that produced them — the same loss pattern seen in corporate M&A when one system replaces another.

## Practical Recommendations

Based on this research, a defensible agent decommissioning and archival practice should include, roughly in sequence:

1. **Inventory and ownership before shutdown.** Confirm the agent has a named owner and a documented scope at decommission time — not retroactively. An agent nobody remembers the purpose of is exactly the "ghost agent" pattern the identity vendors are now building tooling to detect.
2. **Separate the compute/runtime layer from the memory/data layer explicitly.** Shutting off an agent's process does not delete its memory store, vector index, or logs. Each needs its own disposition decision (archive, migrate, or delete) rather than assuming it disappears with the agent.
3. **Tier retention by sensitivity and regulatory exposure, not by convenience.** Default to a hot/cold split (weeks of immediate access, then cold archival) and raise retention for any data category with a specific regulatory floor, rather than applying one retention window to everything an agent touched.
4. **Migrate meaning, not vectors.** If any successor system needs to inherit an archived agent's memory, treat the underlying text/decisions as the portable artifact and let embeddings be regenerated downstream, rather than trying to port a vector store wholesale.
5. **Write a rationale-bearing handoff record, not a raw log export.** A short document capturing what the agent was for, what patterns it learned, and why key decisions were made will outlast and outperform a full conversation dump for any future agent (or human) trying to understand what happened.
6. **Revoke credentials completely and verifiably.** Disable endpoints, revoke tokens/API keys/service-principal grants, and remove the agent from any catalog or registry — with an audit record confirming closure, not just an assumption that deactivation cascaded correctly.
7. **Log the decommissioning itself as an event, in a place a future audit or successor agent can find it.** The absence of a clear "this agent was retired, here's when, why, and by whom" record is precisely what turns a clean shutdown into a future ghost-agent discovery.

None of this is exotic relative to standard IT offboarding discipline — the gap is that it has not yet been applied systematically to agents, whose credentials and memory fan out across more systems than a typical human employee's ever did. The identity-platform tooling that shipped in 2026 (Microsoft Agent 365, Okta's Cross App Access) closes part of this gap for credential and ownership tracking. The archival and knowledge-continuity half of the problem — what to keep, how to structure it, and how a successor inherits it — is still being worked out project by project, which is exactly the position our own zylos100 archival work sits in.
